COMMERCIAL CHECKRIDE — first-hand account from a prior candidate with this DPE

ORAL — airworthiness and documents
- Reviewed aircraft logbooks.
- Asked the required inspections for VFR flight (AV1ATES). Candidate forgot the S, worked it out.
- Asked to determine whether the aircraft was legal by looking back through the logs from a certain page.
- Asked what documents were required for the pilot to fly: license, pilot certificate, medical, photo ID,
  and candidate mentioned Restricted Radiotelephone Operator Permit.

ORAL — go/no-go
- How do you assess risk for a flight (candidate mentioned PAVE and IMSAFE).
- The two types of stress (acute and chronic), with examples of each.
- Required equipment for the aircraft.
- Is a flap position light required? The plane had no KOEL, so candidate used ATOMATOFLAMES and
  verified it was not required.
- Currency versus proficiency.
- What is necessary to be current to carry passengers, and for normal flight, both day and night.

ORAL — holding out, common and private carriage
- Extra context on the cross-country scenario: a customer hires the candidate to fly the customer's own
  airplane; the customer is not a pilot. Can you do this on a commercial certificate? Candidate said yes.
- Then: you arrive and the owner's airplane is leaking oil and is not airworthy. The owner offers to rent
  an airplane and still have you fly it. Is that OK? Candidate raised concerns about the terms of the lease
  and whether the aircraft owner would permit it. DPE pushed on WHO HAS OPERATIONAL CONTROL in that
  situation, making the point that it can get complex, and that when it is complex the right answer is to
  decline or seek official guidance from the FSDO.
- What types of operations can you do with a commercial certificate (banner towing, instructing, local
  sightseeing flights with a letter of authorization, etc.).

ORAL — chart review
- Type of airspace around several airports, from the chart markings.
- Identify services at different airports: fuel available, towered, lighting limitations, runway lengths.
- Equipment required in certain airspace (transponder and ADS-B).
- Requirements to enter various airspace, and the cloud clearances.
- Identify a military training route.
- MOAs and restricted areas.
- Asked about P-51 (national security areas and presidential restricted areas).
- The stadium symbol and its significance (possible TFRs for sporting events).
- What is the ceiling of Class E airspace above a particular airport where Class E begins at 700 ft AGL?
  Took the candidate a minute to get to 18,000 — everything above is controlled and nothing else was above it.
- Power lines, railroad tracks and other chart markings.

ORAL — flight planning
- NOTAMs: where to find them and what types there are.
- Pilot reports and how to obtain them.
- External pressures on the planned trip. Candidate explained the weather was likely IFR, and gave
  alternatives to lower the risk: go IFR, go part-way to where it is still VFR and arrange a car for the
  rest, or do not go. Cited the Kobe Bryant accident as an example of pushing weather to reach a destination.
- Oxygen requirements and how they affected this trip, then the general regulatory requirements. Candidate
  mentioned using a portable pulse oximeter at altitude and having done FAA portable high-altitude training.
- Risks and symptoms of hypoxia; risks and symptoms of hyperventilation; corrective actions for both.

ORAL — weather
- Read a METAR and a TAF; look at several weather charts.
- Define AIRMETs and SIGMETs, what types there are, and what each is issued for.
- Types of clouds.
- Thunderstorms and the risks associated.
- Types of fog.
- The difference between mist and fog.
- What cloud type is low-lying fog (stratus).
- Given theoretical gusty wind data, pick a departure runway and explain why. Determine the crosswind and
  tailwind components for that runway. Would you be comfortable departing in those conditions?
  The wind angle split the two runways evenly. Candidate chose the one with the wind from the LEFT, so as
  not to need more right rudder on takeoff to hold centerline compared with the other runway where the wind
  would be from the right. Explained discomfort because the crosswind component was over 15 knots and
  because of the risk if a return to the airport were needed in an emergency. Explained that the crosswind
  figure in the flight manual is NOT a limitation.

ORAL — systems
- Explain the oil system of the airplane.
- Explain the electrical system.
- Engine type, cylinder configuration, and possibly the ignition system.

ORAL — performance
- Determine takeoff distance, runway length, time to climb and fuel burn for the climb, at both the origin
  and destination airports for the cross-country scenario, given the weather data.
- Explain the cross-country plan: how waypoints were chosen and why. Few follow-ups.
- Show the weight and balance calculation. They were within the envelope but barely — no weight issue, but
  the CG was close to the FORWARD limit. Candidate explained options to correct it, such as putting weight
  or bags in the baggage compartment.
- The impact on stability of a forward CG.

ORAL — other
- The three stages of a stall. CANDIDATE DID NOT KNOW THIS. May have mentioned incipient as the start but
  did not know the official three. The DPE did not belabor it, but then asked him to describe the stall,
  the importance of coordination, and the spin recovery process.
- Reporting an accident, and whether hitting a runway light meets the reporting requirement. Where do you
  find this? The DPE was looking for NTSB Part 830; the candidate found similar guidance in the AIM.

FLIGHT
- Partial sky coverage around 3,000 ft, so they started with landings.
- Briefed the DPE on departure expectations before takeoff, fire extinguisher location, and other items as
  if he were the client. Discussed sterile cockpit procedures and when the highest workload times would be,
  when quiet would be needed. Asked him to speak up if he saw a hazard (birds, other aircraft).
- Short field takeoff, then a short field landing. Candidate was misconfigured (not enough flaps), went
  around, and completed the short field landing to a full stop.
- Soft field takeoff in the pattern to a soft field landing to a full stop.
- Checkride PAUSED for an approaching cell with lightning; resumed about 15 minutes later after it passed.
- Normal takeoff to a power-off 180.
- Departed as if on the cross-country, flew to the first checkpoint, noted the time, identified visual
  references (I-95) and resumed on course using pilotage and heading/bearing.
- Clearing turns, then slow flight: one direction with a turn and climb to a heading and altitude, then the
  other direction with a descent to a given heading.
- Power-on and power-off stalls. Accelerated stall.
- Lazy eights from an entry heading of the candidate's choice. Because of the early cloud layer the DPE said
  they would do lazy eights instead of chandelles; the ceiling lifted but they never switched back and the
  candidate was never asked his preference.
- Steep turns at an entry point, heading and altitude of the candidate's choosing.
- Questioned on emergency procedures, then asked to respond to a simulated engine fire: memory items, fuel
  cut-off, descending at VNE, declaring an emergency, activating the ELT.
- Emergency descent from about 4,000 ft to a simulated approach to land in an open field of his choosing.
  Gave the passenger briefing during the descent once the memory items were complete, then double-checked
  the checklist.
- Recovered from best glide and set up eights on pylons using pylons of his own choosing: a tall power-line
  tower as one pylon, an intersection as the midpoint, and another power-line tower on the far side as the
  other pylon. HE ENTERED INCORRECTLY — into the wind, instead of with a tailwind. Told so on the debrief.
- Asked to determine position on the map using a nearby VOR. Started toward the VOR, then was told they were
  over I-95 and asked to point out their position. Explained the difference between TO and FROM radials and
  used the FROM radial to see where it intercepted I-95.
- After the lost procedure, returned to the airport for a NO-FLAP LANDING as the final landing. Because the
  DPE had already seen an unplanned go-around, he did not give a surprise go-around after a simulated engine
  failure in the pattern.

DEBRIEF
- Main item: taxiing too fast after one of the full-stop landings, and crossing the runway. Doing too many
  things at once (changing a frequency after crossing the runway), briefly lost the centerline and drifted
  left on the taxiway before slowing, recovering and continuing.
- The incorrect entry for the ground reference maneuver: started one maneuver with a headwind instead of a
  tailwind.
